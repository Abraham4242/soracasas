Moving to Haiti in 2026

The honest answer: 2026 is not a feasible or safe time to relocate to Haiti. Every major government warns 'Do Not Travel.' Here is where things actually stand in September, what the international force has and has not achieved, and what would have to change.

By SORA Real Estate Editorial · 9 min read · Updated September 2026
The short version
  • Haiti is under a Level 4 “Do Not Travel” advisory from the US, UK, Canada and Australia, and has been under a national state of emergency since 2024.
  • Roughly 1.5 million people are displaced, about 12% of the population, including 751,000 children.
  • An international Gang Suppression Force was authorised in September 2025 with a ceiling of 5,550 personnel. The first troops did not land until April 2026, and its mandate expires on 30 September.
  • Port-au-Prince is closed to US commercial aviation. Six airports in the north and south remain open.
  • Relocating there is not realistic this year. Below is what we would need to see change before that sentence does.

We will be direct, because the situation demands it: 2026 is not a feasible or safe year to move to Haiti. Every major government maintains its strongest advisory. But “do not go” is where most pages stop, and that is not actually useful to someone with family there, a project there, or a long-term hope of returning. So this page covers where things genuinely stand in September, what the international force has achieved and what it has not, and the specific conditions that would have to change.

The security reality

Armed gangs control an estimated 90% of Port-au-Prince, along with the strategic routes in and out of it and stretches of the border area. The national state of emergency, declared in 2024, now extends beyond the capital into the Artibonite and Centre departments.

The human figures for 2026 are worse than 2025, not better. UN reporting counted more than 3,100 people killed and 1,189 injured by gang violence between January and June alone, with some tallies approaching 4,000. Displacement reached roughly 1.5 million by the end of June, including 751,000 children. The IOM counted 1.47 million internally displaced in May, about 12% of the entire population, with women and girls making up 54% of them.

The Port-au-Prince metropolitan area passed 300,000 displaced residents for the first time this year, driven largely by fighting in Cité Soleil, with most people living in improvised sites rather than organised camps.

What the international force has and hasn't done

This is the part that shifted since our last update, and it is the thing to watch.

On 30 September 2025, the Security Council authorised a Gang Suppression Force under resolution 2793: a non-UN multinational mission with a personnel ceiling of 5,550, more than double the 2,500 envisaged (and never reached) under the previous Multinational Security Support mission. Crucially, the GSF is mandated to actively neutralise, isolate and deter gangs, either alone or alongside the Haitian National Police, rather than merely support them.

That is a genuinely stronger mandate. The execution has lagged badly. The first foreign troops, a Chadian advance team, only arrived on 1 April 2026. And the money tells its own story.

The mandate expires on 30 September 2026. The United States and Panama, the two co-penholders on Haiti at the Security Council, are expected to seek re-authorisation this month, and Haiti's foreign minister Raina Forbin has formally asked the Organization of American States to renew it and fund the full 5,500-strong deployment. Presidential elections are scheduled for December.

Whether that renewal passes, and whether the money follows it, is the single most consequential thing happening in Haiti right now. Assessments so far are honestly mixed: a year on, the gangs still hold the capital.

Getting in and out

Aviation is the clearest measure of how bad things are, because it is decided by regulators rather than by opinion.

The FAA prohibits US commercial flights into Toussaint Louverture International in Port-au-Prince, a restriction first imposed after three US commercial jetliners were struck by gunfire, and repeatedly extended since. US aircraft may only overfly the capital above 10,000 feet.

Six airports remain open to US carriers: Cap-Haïtien, Port-de-Paix, Pignon, Jérémie, Antoine-Simon and Jacmel. That matters practically. Even US government deportation flights land at Cap-Haïtien, more than 80 miles north of the capital, because Washington judges Port-au-Prince too dangerous for its own aircraft. If the US will not land its own charters there, that answers the question about your commercial itinerary.

Land and sea crossings with the Dominican Republic remain largely closed and unreliable.

The returnee picture

One thing has changed sharply for the Haitian diaspora, and it belongs on this page even though it is not relocation in the usual sense.

Temporary Protected Status for Haitians has ended, and US deportation flights have moved from roughly monthly to weekly. Nearly 200,000 people were forcibly returned to Haiti in the first eight months of 2026, about 10% more than the same period last year, arriving into a country with 1.5 million people already displaced inside it.

If you or your family are affected by the end of TPS, this is a legal and immigration question rather than a relocation one, and it needs a qualified immigration attorney rather than a real estate guide.

Why relocation isn't practical

  • Foreigners are targets. Kidnapping for ransom is a primary gang revenue stream and affects every demographic. Ransom demands commonly run $50,000 to $200,000.
  • No reliable way in. The capital's airport is closed to US carriers, and the land borders are largely shut.
  • No safety net. US consular capacity is extremely limited and the UK suspended consular services in 2025. No commercial insurance covers evacuation from Haiti, and medical evacuation is prohibitively expensive when it is available at all.
  • Collapsed services. Gang control of fuel terminals drives nationwide shortages that reach hospitals, schools and water supply.
  • Nothing to buy into safely. Property rights depend on functioning courts and registries. Neither is reliably available, and a title you cannot enforce is not an asset.

What would have to change

We would rather give you a watchlist than a flat refusal, so here is what we are actually tracking before this page reads differently:

  • The GSF mandate is renewed and funded to something near its 5,550 ceiling, with disbursed money rather than pledges.
  • The FAA and other regulators reopen Port-au-Prince to commercial traffic. This is the cleanest single indicator, because it is a technical judgement about whether aircraft get shot at.
  • Major governments move Haiti off Level 4. A shift to Level 3 would be the first formal signal in years.
  • December's presidential elections actually happen, and produce a government with functioning institutions behind it.
  • Displacement figures fall rather than plateau. Return, not just a slowing of departure.

None of these are speculative wishes. They are all measurable, and they are all published. Watch them yourself rather than taking anyone's word for the mood.

Safer alternatives

Level 4 · Do Not Travel is the highest advisory level. The US, UK, Canada and Australia all apply it to Haiti, which has been under a national state of emergency since 2024.

If what you want is Caribbean relocation, the same island offers the Dominican Republic: affordable, welcoming, and with a straightforward residency path. Jamaica and the rest of the region are covered across our island-by-island guide.

If your connection to Haiti is cultural or familial rather than geographic, the Dominican Republic, the Bahamas and south Florida all host substantial Haitian communities and sit within reach of the northern airports that remain open. That is a genuinely different proposition from relocating into the crisis, and for a lot of people it is the honest middle path.

Frequently asked questions

Can you move to Haiti in 2026?

Realistically, no. Haiti carries a Level 4 “Do Not Travel” advisory from the US, UK, Canada and Australia, and has been under a national state of emergency since 2024. Armed gangs control around 90% of the capital, foreigners are targeted for kidnapping, Port-au-Prince is closed to US commercial flights, borders are largely shut, consular services are minimal, and no commercial insurance covers evacuation.

Is the international security force working?

Too early to say, and the record so far is mixed. The Gang Suppression Force was authorised in September 2025 with a ceiling of 5,550 personnel and a real mandate to fight gangs rather than just support police, but the first troops only landed in April 2026 and just $67.5 million of $223.5 million pledged had been disbursed by July. Its mandate expires on 30 September 2026, and renewal is the thing to watch.

Which airports in Haiti are open?

Six are open to US carriers: Cap-Haïtien, Port-de-Paix, Pignon, Jérémie, Antoine-Simon and Jacmel. Toussaint Louverture in Port-au-Prince is prohibited to US commercial aviation after jetliners were struck by gunfire, and US aircraft may only overfly the capital above 10,000 feet. Even US deportation flights land at Cap-Haïtien instead.

What has happened since TPS ended?

US deportation flights to Haiti moved from roughly monthly to weekly, and nearly 200,000 people were forcibly returned in the first eight months of 2026, about 10% more than the same period a year earlier. If your family is affected, that is an immigration law question and warrants a qualified attorney rather than relocation advice.

What is a safer alternative in the Caribbean?

The Dominican Republic, which shares the island of Hispaniola, is affordable, welcoming and has an easy residency path. It also hosts a large Haitian community, as do the Bahamas and south Florida, which matters if your connection is cultural rather than geographic.
