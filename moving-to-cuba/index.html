Moving to Cuba in 2026

The Caribbean's largest island is one of its most fascinating — and one of the hardest to actually move to. Here's the honest reality on residency, cost, healthcare, and the extra US-sanctions layer for Americans.

By SORA Real Estate Editorial · 8 min read · Updated July 2026

Let's be honest up front: Cuba is not set up for immigration. It's the largest, in many ways most captivating island in the Caribbean — extraordinary music, architecture, and community — but there is no standard residency, retirement, or digital-nomad route. If you're picturing a relaxed retirement move like the DR or Panama, Cuba isn't that. Here's what's actually possible, and why.

Can you actually move to Cuba?

Level 2 · Exercise Increased Caution — everyday crime is moderate, but Cuba's deeper challenges are economic — shortages, blackouts, and (for Americans) US sanctions — more than street safety.

For most people, no — not in the ordinary sense. Cuba maintains tight state control over foreign residence, with no publicly advertised programs for retirees, investors, or remote workers. Long-term residency is possible but narrow and bureaucratically opaque.

The narrow legal paths

  • Family ties: marriage to a Cuban citizen, or having a child born on the island (the most reliable route).
  • Work or study: a contract with a state enterprise or approved foreign company, or enrolment at a Cuban institution.
  • Otherwise: you can string together tourist stays — up to about three months via monthly extensions — but that's visiting, not living.

Permanent residency (residencia permanente), once granted, does confer real benefits — near-citizen rights, access to healthcare, the ability to be self-employed and buy a vehicle or property — but qualifying is the hard part. Document/translation costs alone run around $1,500.

Cost of living: the dual-market catch

Cuba looks cheap until you realize expats rarely pay local prices. There are effectively two economies — a subsidized local market and a dollar-indexed “expat/tourist” market you'll mostly live in. In Havana, a foreigner realistically spends around €1,300/month; frugal living in smaller cities can be far less. Foreigners can only rent (or buy from other foreigners), not buy from Cubans.

Healthcare & daily friction

Cuba's famous free healthcare largely doesn't apply to foreigners, who are directed to paid “international” clinics — and chronic medicine shortages mean many foreign residents fly to Mexico once or twice a year to restock prescriptions. Add unreliable, expensive internet and frequent blackouts, and independent expat life is genuinely difficult.

For Americans: the sanctions layer

US citizens face a second legal system entirely. Travel is legal only under one of 12 permitted categories (tourism isn't one), OFAC bars transactions with a long list of restricted government-owned hotels and stores, and there is no US sanctions category for permanently relocating to Cuba. Policy also swings with each administration. Any American considering it must navigate both Cuban immigration and OFAC compliance carefully.

Bottom line

Cuba is best understood as a place for short cultural stays or those with genuine family ties — not a conventional expat or retirement destination. If a Caribbean move is your goal, the Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico, or the eastern-Caribbean islands will get you there far more easily.

Frequently asked questions

Can Americans move to Cuba?

Not in the ordinary sense. Cuba has no standard residency, retirement, or digital-nomad program — long-term residency generally requires marriage to a Cuban, a child born on the island, or a work/study arrangement. Americans also face a separate layer of US sanctions: travel is allowed only under 12 permitted categories (tourism isn't one), and there is no US sanctions category for permanently relocating to Cuba.

How much does it cost to live in Cuba as a foreigner?

It looks cheap but expats rarely pay local prices — you'll mostly live in a dollar-indexed 'tourist' market. In Havana a foreigner realistically spends around €1,300/month; smaller cities are cheaper. Foreigners can only rent, or buy property from other foreigners, not from Cubans.

What is healthcare like for foreigners in Cuba?

Cuba's free public system largely doesn't apply to foreigners, who use paid 'international' clinics. Chronic medicine shortages are a serious issue — many foreign residents travel to Mexico once or twice a year to restock prescriptions — so comprehensive insurance and planning are essential.
