Concrete Flat Roofs (Losa)

The monolithic reinforced-concrete slab — Panama's permanent, hurricane-proof roof that doubles as a terrace. Extremely durable; its whole game is waterproofing and drainage.

By SORA Real Estate Editorial · 6 min read · Updated July 2026

Across Panama and much of Latin America, the default roof isn't tile or shingle — it's a flat reinforced-concrete slab, the losa. It's the same reinforced concrete the walls are built from, poured as a monolithic deck: virtually indestructible, completely fireproof, impervious to termites, and about as hurricane-proof as a roof gets. It also gives you a flat surface you can walk on — a terrace, a rooftop garden, a future second floor. The one thing a concrete roof demands, relentlessly, is waterproofing and drainage.

How it's built

A slab roof is formed and poured like any suspended concrete floor: formwork, a grid of steel rebar, then concrete, cured and stripped. Because it's flat (or very low-slope), water doesn't run off on its own, so the whole system depends on a waterproof membrane (sheet, liquid, or acrylic system), a slight built-in slope, and drains/scuppers that move tropical downpours off fast. Insulation and a reflective top coat are added to stop the mass of concrete from soaking up and holding heat.

What does it cost?

Concrete slab roofs are $$$ in materials and labor — more than a metal or tin roof upfront — roughly $90–170/m² ($8–16/sq ft) for the structural slab plus waterproofing, though costs blur because the roof is integral to the building's concrete structure. You're paying for permanence and usable space. Over a 50+-year life with good waterproofing maintenance, the value is strong. (Approximate; span, thickness, and finish drive it.)

Buildability — how hard is it to actually build?

A concrete slab roof is squarely a professional job — it's structural engineering, formwork, rebar, and a concrete pour — but it's also the roof local builders across Panama know best, because it's how a huge share of homes here are already built. The technical risk isn't the concrete; it's the waterproofing and drainage detailing, which is where flat roofs fail when they fail. Insist on a proper membrane system, adequate slope to the drains, and a reflective/insulated top layer so the slab doesn't turn into a heat sink. Done right, it's the most permanent, storm-immune roof you can build — and the only one that hands you an extra floor of living space.

Frequently asked questions

Do flat concrete roofs leak?

Only when the waterproofing fails. The concrete slab itself is durable and impervious, but because it's flat, water can't run off on its own — so the roof depends entirely on a sound waterproof membrane, a slight slope, and working drains. Maintained properly (periodic re-coating), a concrete roof stays dry for decades.

Is a concrete roof good in hurricanes?

Exceptionally — a monolithic reinforced-concrete slab has nothing to lift, tear, or blow away, so it's among the most storm-resistant roofs possible. It's also fireproof and termite-proof, which adds to its appeal in the tropics.

Does a concrete flat roof make the house hot?

It can, because concrete's thermal mass absorbs and holds heat. The fix is standard practice: insulation above or below the slab and a reflective (often white acrylic) top coat, which together keep the mass from radiating heat into the rooms below.
