Prefab & Modular Homes

Factory-built homes assembled on site, faster and more consistent than stick-building. What they cost, the trade-offs, and whether they work in the tropics.

By SORA Real Estate Editorial · 6 min read · Updated September 2026
The short version
  • Modular homes run $80 to $175 per square foot installed. Savings against site-built are real but slim, usually 10 to 20%.
  • The factory quote is not the price. Land, foundation, utilities, driveway and permits cost the same either way, and site work alone can run $30,000 to well over $100,000.
  • Delivery adds $5 to $10 per square foot, so $3,000 to $20,000 before a module is set.
  • Do not confuse modular with manufactured. Modular meets the same local building codes as a site-built house. That distinction decides your financing and your resale.
  • In Panama and Costa Rica the deciding question is not price. It is whether a truck carrying a room can reach your lot.

Prefab homes are built as finished sections in a factory, trucked to your land, and assembled on a foundation. The pitch is compelling: a build 30 to 50% faster than site construction, made indoors on jigs where rain never stops work and a tape measure never lies. Most of that pitch holds up. The part that gets oversold is the money, so let's deal with that honestly.

How they're made

Prefab is the umbrella. Underneath it sit three different things, and knowing which one you are buying matters more than any brochure:

  • Modular is the most complete form. Whole three-dimensional room modules, kitchens and bathrooms included, are finished in a factory, shipped, then craned onto a permanent foundation. Crucially, modular homes must meet the same local and state building codes as a site-built house.
  • Panelized and kit homes ship flat wall and roof panels for fast framing on site. Roughly $100 to $200 per square foot all-in.
  • Manufactured homes are built on a permanent steel chassis to the federal HUD code rather than local codes. They are far cheaper, around $40 to $85 per square foot, and they are a different product entirely with different financing, different zoning treatment and different resale behaviour.

Almost every eye-catching prefab savings statistic you have read is a statistic about manufactured homes. Manufactured averages $55 to $65 per square foot against roughly $168 for site-built nationally. Modular does not get anywhere near that gap, and anyone quoting you those numbers for a modular build is comparing two different things.

The cost

Modular homes land between $80 and $175 per square foot fully installed, which puts a 2,000 square foot house somewhere between $160,000 and $350,000. Base modules alone are $50 to $100 per square foot before site work, foundation, utilities and finishing.

Notice how much is sitting after the word "before". This is where prefab budgets go to die.

That $87,000 covered clearing, a basement dug into rocky soil, a 320-foot well and a mound septic system. None of it was in the factory quote, and none of it would have been cheaper with a site-built house.

Add delivery on top. Transport runs $5 to $10 per square foot, or $3,000 to $20,000 for a typical house, and it climbs fast with distance and road difficulty.

So where does the saving actually come from? Labour, and only labour. Because so much work happens in the factory, the general contractor's share falls to roughly 20 to 40% of the project. Factories also cut material waste by around a quarter. Those are real gains, worth about 10 to 20% against a comparable site build. They are not the 50% the internet promises, and they vanish entirely if your site is difficult.

The practical advice: price your site work before you fall in love with a factory quote. Get the well drilled, the soil tested and the access road assessed first. Our 2026 Panama build cost guide covers what those line items look like locally.

Can you build it in Panama or Costa Rica?

Yes, and on the right lot it is excellent. Several regional builders offer concrete or steel modular systems that shrug off humidity and termites, and the speed and consistency are real advantages in a market where site labour quality swings widely.

The friction here is not price or code. It is the road.

A module is a room on a truck. It is wide, tall, heavy and unforgiving about corners. Getting one up the switchbacks to a highland lot above Boquete, or across a bridge with a posted weight limit in Chiriquí, is a logistics question you have to answer before you sign anything. Then the crane has to reach the site too, and set up somewhere reasonably level.

Our rule of thumb: if a fully loaded cement truck can reach your building pad without drama, modular is worth pricing. If it cannot, the module will not get there either, and you are back to building in place.

One more local wrinkle. Ask what the modules are made of. A wood-framed module built for a dry North American market and shipped into the tropics arrives with the same humidity and termite exposure as any other timber house. Concrete or steel modules are the ones that suit the climate. If you want the full comparison, the building methods index lines them all up, and ICF is the usual alternative when the road says no.

Frequently asked questions

Are modular homes cheaper in practice?

Modestly, around 10 to 20% against a comparable site-built house, and it comes only from saved labour. Land, foundation, utilities, driveway and permits cost the same either way, and site work can run from $30,000 to over $100,000. Price your site before you price your house.

What is the difference between modular and manufactured?

Modular homes are built in sections and assembled on a permanent foundation, and must meet the same local building codes as a site-built house. Manufactured homes sit on a permanent steel chassis and follow the federal HUD code instead. That difference drives financing, zoning and resale, and most headline prefab savings figures are manufactured-home figures.

Are prefab and modular homes cheaper?

Modestly, sometimes, the bigger, more reliable saving is speed (often months faster). Material and labor savings from factory efficiency can be offset by shipping and craning modules to the site, especially on remote or mountainous lots.

Do modular homes hold up in hurricanes and earthquakes?

Engineered modular homes with proper connections perform well; concrete and steel modules especially. The module-to-module and module-to-foundation connections are the critical detail.

Is a prefab home easy to finance and insure?

Generally yes, far easier than earthen or experimental methods. Codes and banks understand modular construction, so permitting, mortgaging, and insurance are usually straightforward.
