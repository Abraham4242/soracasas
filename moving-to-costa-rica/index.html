Moving to Costa Rica

An honest 2026 guide to relocating to Costa Rica — the real cost of living (higher than people expect), how safe it is, the residency routes, healthcare, and where the pura-vida life actually works best.

By SORA Real Estate Editorial · 12 min read · Updated July 2026

Costa Rica sells itself on pura vida — a genuinely stable democracy (no army since 1948), lush nature, universal healthcare, and one of the happiest populations on earth. It's the most established expat destination in Central America. The honest catch most guides skip: it's also more expensive than people expect — often pricier than Panama or Mexico. Here's the real picture: what it costs, how safe it is, how to stay, and where it works best.

Why people move to Costa Rica

  • Stability — a peaceful, long-standing democracy with no military and strong institutions.
  • Nature & biodiversity — ~5% of the world's species, national parks everywhere, two coastlines and cloud forests.
  • Universal healthcare — the public "Caja" system, plus excellent affordable private care.
  • Established expat infrastructure — decades of relocations mean services, communities, and know-how are everywhere.
  • The pura-vida culture — a genuinely relaxed, friendly, environmentally-minded way of life.
  • Climate variety — hot coasts, and cool, spring-like Central Valley highlands.

Cost of living in Costa Rica

Here's the honest part: Costa Rica is not the bargain many assume. Import taxes, a strong colón, and its popularity have pushed prices up — a comfortable couple typically needs $2,500–3,500/month, with tourist-heavy coastal areas (Tamarindo, Nosara) higher and inland towns (San Isidro, the Central Valley) more affordable. Is Costa Rica safe?

Costa Rica is one of the safer countries in Latin America and a very comfortable place for expats, though it's worth being clear-eyed: property crime and opportunistic theft (break-ins, things left in cars, beach-bag grabs) are the real day-to-day risk, and rates have risen with tourism in some hotspots. Violent crime remains low by regional standards and rarely touches the expat lifestyle. Use normal precautions — don't flash valuables, secure your home, be alert in tourist zones — and most expats feel very safe.

Visas & residency

Costa Rica offers several established routes:

  • Pensionado — for retirees with a lifetime pension of ~$1,000/month.
  • Rentista — prove a stable income of ~$2,500/month (or a qualifying deposit) for a couple of years; popular with the not-yet-retired.
  • Inversionista (investor) — a qualifying investment (often in property or a business) of a set minimum.
  • Digital Nomad Visa — for remote workers meeting an income threshold, letting you stay long-term without full residency.

All eventually open access to the public healthcare system and a path toward permanent residency. Confirm current thresholds with a Costa Rican immigration attorney — the numbers move.

Healthcare

Healthcare is a major draw. The public Caja (CCSS) system provides universal coverage to legal residents for an income-based monthly contribution, and the private system is excellent and affordable, with top hospitals in San José (some serving a real medical-tourism market). Many expats keep Caja for major coverage and pay out-of-pocket or hold private insurance for faster, more comfortable private care. Overall quality is high and costs are a fraction of the US.

Where do expats actually live?

  • Central Valley (Atenas, Grecia, Escazú, San Ramón) — spring-like climate, close to San José and the airport and top hospitals; the most popular expat base.
  • San Isidro & the Valley of El General — a real Costa Rican city in a fertile valley, a fraction of the coast's land cost; see our full guide.
  • Southern Pacific (Dominical, Uvita, Ojochal) — jungle-meets-beach, whale watching, a growing community.
  • Guanacaste / North Pacific (Tamarindo, Nosara, Playas del Coco) — the beach-and-surf expat coast; beautiful, drier, and pricier.
  • Lake Arenal — lush, cooler, lake-and-volcano scenery with a tight expat community.

The honest downsides

  • Costlier than expected — the big one; don't assume Central-American bargain prices.
  • Bureaucracy ("trámites") — famously slow; residency, utilities, and imports test your patience.
  • Property crime — the main safety issue; secure your home and belongings.
  • Rainy season is intense — the green season (roughly May–November) brings serious rain, especially on the coasts.
  • Infrastructure gaps — rural roads, occasional power/water cuts, and "tico time" are part of the deal.

How to actually make the move

  1. Scout in the green season — visit candidate areas during the rains to see the real climate.
  2. Rent for 6–12 months — before buying anywhere; regions differ enormously.
  3. Pick a residency route — with a Costa Rican immigration attorney (Pensionado, Rentista, Nomad, or Inversionista).
  4. Register with the Caja & sort private care — once a resident.
  5. Buy inland for value — land away from the tourist coasts stretches your budget dramatically.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to live in Costa Rica?

A comfortable couple typically needs $2,500–3,500 per month, covering rent, food, utilities, healthcare, and transport — more in tourist-heavy coastal areas like Tamarindo or Nosara, and less inland (e.g. the Central Valley or the San Isidro area). Costa Rica is pricier than many expect, largely due to import taxes and its popularity.

Is Costa Rica safe for expats?

Yes, relatively — it's one of the safer countries in Latin America, with low violent crime. The real day-to-day risk is property crime and opportunistic theft, especially in tourist areas, so securing your home and belongings and using normal precautions is important. Most expats feel very safe living there.

What are the residency options for Costa Rica?

The main routes are Pensionado (retirees with a ~$1,000/month pension), Rentista (proof of ~$2,500/month income or a qualifying deposit), Inversionista (a qualifying investment), and the Digital Nomad Visa (for remote workers meeting an income threshold). Thresholds change, so confirm current requirements with a Costa Rican immigration attorney.

Is healthcare good in Costa Rica?

Yes — Costa Rica has universal public healthcare (the Caja/CCSS) available to legal residents for an income-based contribution, plus an excellent, affordable private system with top hospitals in San José. Many expats combine both, and overall quality is high at a fraction of US costs.

Is Costa Rica or Panama cheaper to live in?

Panama is generally cheaper than Costa Rica, especially in Panama's interior towns, and it uses the US dollar. Costa Rica tends to cost more due to import taxes and its popularity, though it offers universal healthcare and famous stability. See our companion guide, Moving to Panama, to compare directly.
