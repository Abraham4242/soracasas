Moving to Panama

A complete, honest 2026 guide to relocating to Panama — real cost of living, how safe it actually is, the residency routes that make it easy, healthcare, and where expats actually choose to live.

By SORA Real Estate Editorial · 12 min read · Updated July 2026

Panama has quietly become one of the easiest, most practical places in the world for a North American or European to relocate. It uses the US dollar, sits outside the hurricane belt, has one of the most welcoming residency programs anywhere, and offers everything from Caribbean beaches to cool, spring-like mountain towns — all a short flight from the US. This is the honest, up-to-date guide: what it costs, how safe it really is, how you get to stay, and where you'd actually want to live.

Why people move to Panama

  • The dollar economy — no currency risk; your savings and income are already in the local currency.
  • Easy residency — the Friendly Nations and Pensionado programs are among the most accessible in the world (more below).
  • Outside the hurricane belt — unlike much of the Caribbean, mainland Panama rarely sees direct hurricanes.
  • Climate for every taste — hot beaches at sea level, and eternal-spring mountain towns like Boquete in the highlands.
  • Proximity & connectivity — 3–5 hours to most US hubs, a modern airport, and good internet in the main areas.
  • Modern infrastructure — Panama City is the most developed capital in Central America, with world-class private hospitals.

Cost of living in Panama

Panama is cheaper than the US, but not the cheapest country in Latin America — and the range is huge depending on where and how you live. A retired couple living modestly in the interior (Boquete, Coronado, David) can live well on $2,000–2,800/month; Panama City is noticeably pricier; a frugal single person in the interior can manage on far less. Is Panama safe?

For an expat living in the normal places, Panama is one of the safer countries in Latin America — comparable to a mid-sized US city, and the mountain and beach towns where most expats settle are notably calm. As anywhere, there's petty crime in parts of Panama City and areas to avoid (Colón, the Darién near the Colombian border), and normal urban common sense applies. Violent crime rates are well below the regional average, tourism infrastructure is mature, and the large, established expat communities are a good sign of day-to-day safety. It is not a place most expats feel unsafe.

Visas & residency — the easy part

This is Panama's superpower. Two programs stand out:

  • Friendly Nations Visa — citizens of ~50 countries (US, Canada, most of Europe, and more) can get permanent residency, historically via a local economic tie (a property purchase of a set minimum, or a job). Fast, popular, and a path to eventual citizenship.
  • Pensionado (retiree) Visa — prove a lifetime pension of ~$1,000/month and you get residency plus Panama's famous retiree discounts (on flights, medical, restaurants, and more). One of the best retiree programs anywhere.
  • Qualified Investor Visa — a faster, investment-based route (see our QIV guide).

Rules and minimums change, so confirm the current requirements with a Panamanian immigration attorney — but the headline is real: Panama wants foreign residents and has built the programs to match.

Healthcare

Panama has good, affordable healthcare, with excellent private hospitals in Panama City (several affiliated with US institutions) and solid regional hospitals in David and other hubs. Private care costs a fraction of the US, and private insurance for an older couple typically runs a few hundred dollars a month. Many expats use a mix of affordable out-of-pocket private care and a hospitalization policy. In the interior, you're rarely far from competent care, and Panama City is the region's medical capital.

Where do expats actually live?

Panama packs a lot of very different places into a small country:

  • Boquete (Chiriquí highlands) — the flagship expat town: eternal spring, Geisha coffee, a big established community, no AC needed. See our complete Boquete guide.
  • Volcán & Cerro Punta — cooler, greener, cheaper highland alternatives to Boquete (Volcán real estate).
  • Pedasí & the Azuero coast — laid-back beach-town life; see Boquete vs Pedasí.
  • Coronado & the Pacific beaches — the closest beach expat hub to the capital, very convenient.
  • Bocas del Toro — Caribbean-island living, funky and beautiful (and a bit more rustic).
  • Panama City — for those who want a real cosmopolitan capital: skyline, restaurants, top hospitals, and an international airport.

Buying or building a home

Foreigners can own property outright in Panama (titled land), and building your own home is very achievable — often for less than buying finished. Start with our 2026 cost-to-build breakdown, our guide to buying land as a foreigner, and how to vet a custom home builder. Building in reinforced concrete (the regional standard) gives you a durable, insurable, storm-ready home.

The honest downsides

  • Bureaucracy is slow — "mañana" is real; paperwork, utilities, and permits take patience.
  • The rainy season is wet — roughly May–November, with daily downpours (the highlands stay green and lush for it).
  • Not everywhere is cheap — Panama City and imported goods can surprise you; the savings are real but selective.
  • Spanish helps a lot — you can get by with English in expat hubs, but life is far easier with some Spanish.
  • Infrastructure varies — power and internet are good in the hubs, patchier in remote spots.

How to actually make the move

  1. Visit first, in both seasons — spend real time in 2–3 candidate towns, ideally including the rainy season.
  2. Rent before you buy — live in an area for 6–12 months before committing to property.
  3. Engage a Panamanian immigration attorney — to pick and file the right residency route.
  4. Sort banking and healthcare — open a local account and line up private insurance.
  5. Then buy or build — once you know the area, secure land and a home (SORA can help here).

Frequently asked questions

How much money do you need to live comfortably in Panama?

A retired couple living modestly in the interior (Boquete, Coronado, David) can live comfortably on roughly $2,000–2,800 per month, covering rent, food, utilities, healthcare, and transport. Panama City costs more, and a frugal single person can spend considerably less. Heavy air-conditioning use and imported goods are the main budget-inflators.

Is Panama safe to live in?

For expats in the usual areas, yes — Panama is among the safer countries in Latin America, with violent crime well below the regional average and calm, established expat communities in the mountain and beach towns. Normal urban caution applies in parts of Panama City, and a few areas (Colón, the Darién border) are best avoided.

What is the easiest way to get residency in Panama?

The Friendly Nations Visa (for citizens of ~50 countries, typically via a property purchase or local economic tie) and the Pensionado Visa (for retirees with a ~$1,000/month pension) are among the most accessible residency programs in the world. Requirements change, so confirm current rules with a Panamanian immigration attorney.

Does Panama use the US dollar?

Yes — Panama uses the US dollar as its currency (its own balboa is pegged 1:1 and mostly exists as coins). That means no currency risk for dollar-earners and dollar-savers, a major reason it's popular with American expats and retirees.

Do you need to speak Spanish to live in Panama?

You can get by with English in the main expat hubs (Boquete, Coronado, Panama City), where many services cater to foreigners. But everyday life — dealing with utilities, healthcare, officialdom, and locals — is far smoother with at least basic Spanish, and it's strongly recommended.
