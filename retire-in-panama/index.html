Retire in Panama

Real 2026 budgets, six towns compared honestly, and the one deadline that has nothing to do with visas.

By SORA Real Estate Editorial ·14 min read ·Updated August 2026

Panama has been sold to retirees for twenty years on four facts that happen to be true: it uses the US dollar, it does not tax your foreign pension, the retiree visa needs only $1,000 a month, and Miami is under three hours away. What the brochures leave out is that the decision with the shortest deadline is not the visa. It is health insurance, and it closes at 75.

At a glance

  • A couple lives well on $2,500 to $3,000 a month in Boquete or Coronado. Honest all-in, with insurance, flights home and a vehicle fund, is closer to $4,000.
  • The visa threshold is not a budget. Pensionado asks $1,000 a month of pension income. That qualifies you. It does not house you.
  • Most insurers stop taking new members at 75, some at 64. This is the real timing constraint on retiring here.
  • The highlands need no air conditioning. Boquete sits between 58°F and 78°F all year, and the power bill shows it.
  • No local tax on foreign pensions. Panama taxes Panama-source income only. Americans still file with the IRS.
  • Rent first. A year in the wrong town is a bad year. A house in the wrong town is a bad decade.

What it actually costs

Published Panama budgets tend to describe a couple who rent modestly, cook at home, own no car and never fly anywhere. That couple exists. Most people are not them.

The planning number adds what does not arrive monthly: two flights home a year, a vehicle you will eventually replace, roof and appliance repairs, and US tax preparation, which for an expat return is not cheap. Add those and a comfortable couple lands at $48,000 to $55,000 a year. Panama City pushes the same lifestyle to $60,000 to $72,000.

The pensionado

Law 6 of 1987 created the most generous statutory retiree discount schedule of any residency programme anywhere. It requires $1,000 a month in lifetime pension income, plus $250 a month per dependant, so a couple needs $1,250. It drops to $750 if you also own Panamanian property worth $100,000 or more. There is no minimum age, which catches people by surprise. A 45 year old with a qualifying pension can hold pensionado residency.

Used routinely, a couple recovers $150 to $350 a month. That is real money against a $3,000 budget, and it is the reason the pensionado beats the other routes for anyone who qualifies.

The word doing the work is pension. It must come from a government, a company scheme, a trust, a mutual fund, a bank or an insurance company, and it must be for life. Rental income does not count. Investment income does not count. A 401(k) balance does not count, though an annuity purchased from it does. This is where most failed applications die, and people usually find out after they have already moved. The residency requirements guide covers the alternatives if you do not qualify.

The age wall

This is the section that should change your timeline, and it is missing from almost every retire-in-Panama article.

Most Panamanian insurers stop accepting new members at 75. Several stop between 64 and 70. Local plans routinely exclude pre-existing conditions, in some cases for a full two year waiting period. Premiums run roughly $50 to $310 a month per adult depending on age and health profile, and rise steeply through the sixties.

The number is not really the problem. The interaction is. A healthy 68 year old with controlled blood pressure gets covered without argument. A 74 year old with a prior stroke and diabetes gets exclusions, a quote nobody would pay, or a refusal. Conditions accumulate faster than birthdays.

If you arrive uninsurable

It is not the end of the plan, but it is a different plan.

The public CSS system has no age limit and no pre-existing condition limit. It accepts everyone. Doctor visits run around $2 and specialists around $5. The trade is queues and Spanish.

Low-cost membership plans such as MiniMed run about $22 a month with no age or condition exclusion. Paired with a high-deductible international policy for catastrophic cover, this is the standard workaround.

Cash private care is affordable. A private GP visit is $30 to $60 and a specialist $50 to $100. Many Panamanian doctors trained abroad and speak English. For routine care, paying cash is genuinely viable.

The honest read: if you are 55 or 60 and considering Panama in ten years, buy the insurance when you arrive, not when you need it. If you are 72, this is the item to solve before the house.

The tax part

Panama is territorial. It taxes income sourced in Panama and leaves foreign income alone. Your US Social Security, your pension, your dividends from a US brokerage are not taxed by Panama.

Two things people get wrong. First, US citizens file US returns regardless of where they live, and the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion does not apply to pension or investment income, so this is a Panama tax saving rather than a total one. Second, if you rent out property in Panama, that income is Panama-source and taxable there. Property tax is exempt on the first $120,000 of value on a primary residence, and new construction can carry an exemption that transfers with the property when sold.

What goes wrong

Five failures account for most of the people who leave within two years.

  • Buying in month two. Panama is a slow, thin resale market outside the capital. Buying takes a weekend, selling takes a year. Rent for twelve months, through a full rainy season, before you commit anything.
  • Underestimating the rain. Boquete gets a persistent fine mist locals call bajareque, and the wet season runs May to December. People who visited in February are frequently unprepared.
  • Assuming income qualifies. Rental and investment income do not meet the pensionado test at any size.
  • Treating a Rights of Possession property as ownership. Untitled land is common in the highlands and does not carry title. It is a real risk with a real solution, which is titling, and it takes time.
  • Waiting on the insurance. Covered above. It is the one item that gets strictly harder every year and cannot be fixed with money past a certain point.
