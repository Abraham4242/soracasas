Shipping Container Homes

Steel boxes turned into modern homes, cheaper on paper than you'd think once you finish them properly, and genuinely tricky in tropical heat. The honest breakdown.

By SORA Real Estate Editorial · 9 min read · Updated September 2026
The short version
  • A used cargo-worthy 40 ft high cube is $1,800–$3,500. A one-trip is $3,500–$5,500, a 40 to 60% premium for a box with no rust history.
  • Delivery is not a rounding error: $800–$1,500 under 50 miles, and past $4,000 cross-country.
  • Insulation, openings and systems add 30 to 60% on top. Realistic all-in per box before foundation, permits or finish: $9,000–$14,000.
  • In a container home the steel is the structure. Corrosion is not cosmetic, and spray foam applied over rusty steel hides the problem while it grows.
  • Panama has one genuine advantage here, and it is sitting at the end of the Canal.

A used shipping container costs a few thousand dollars, which is why container homes look almost free. They are not. The steel box is the cheapest part of the project, and in a hot, salty, humid climate it is also the part that will try to fail on you. Here is what the numbers actually look like, and the one failure mode you should understand before you commit.

How they go together

Standard 20 ft or 40 ft containers get cut for windows and doors, reinforced wherever steel was removed, insulated, clad and fitted out. Boxes stack and combine for more space.

Two things to know up front. Every opening you cut weakens a structure that was engineered as a stressed steel skin, so reinforcement is not optional. And a well-insulated container is essentially airtight, which means ventilation has to be designed in. Without it you get CO₂ build-up and condensation in an occupied room.

What a box really costs

Start with the grade, because the words matter. Cargo-worthy means a licensed maritime surveyor certified the box structurally sound for ocean shipping. It is a structural certification, not a cosmetic one. Expect 8 to 15 years of age, faded paint, surface rust and dents up to about 2.5 inches deep. A CW 40 ft high cube runs $1,800–$3,500.

A one-trip container has made exactly one voyage from the factory. Undamaged floors, good door seals, factory paint, a clean CSC plate. A one-trip 40 ft high cube runs $3,500–$5,500, a premium of 40 to 60%. For a home, that premium buys you a box with no corrosion history you have to inherit, which is worth more than it sounds.

Then delivery, which people forget entirely. Under 50 miles is $800–$1,500. Fifty to 200 miles is $1,500–$2,500. Long hauls exceed $4,000. The same used 20 ft that costs $1,300 near a port sells for $2,400 inland, and the whole difference is trucking.

The rust you cannot see

This is the section worth reading twice.

A container is a monocoque: the corrugated steel skin is the structure. There is no separate frame holding it up. So corrosion is never a cosmetic issue. It is a structural one, and in coastal salt air it runs faster than most people expect.

Now the trap. The standard advice is to insulate with closed-cell spray foam, and for humid climates that advice is right — open-cell foam absorbs moisture over time and loses its R-value, while closed-cell acts as its own vapour barrier and stops the condensation that forms when warm room air meets cold steel.

But foam applied over untreated or already-rusting steel seals the corrosion in and hides it from view. It keeps spreading behind the foam, invisible, until it is a structural problem rather than a maintenance one. As one coatings manufacturer put it: spray foam is not the enemy, blind faith in it is.

Three things follow from that, and they are all cheap compared to the alternative:

  • Treat and coat every steel surface before any insulation touches it. Not some of it. All of it.
  • Attack heat at the outside first. High-reflectance, high-emissivity exterior coatings lower peak steel temperature and reduce thermal cycling, which reduces both the cooling load and the condensation driving force. In a solar-dominated climate this does more than adding R-value inside.
  • Check what is already on the steel. Many containers carry marine paints that are genuinely toxic, and foam can trap VOCs that would otherwise off-gas away. Specify closed-cell, low-VOC, and strip or seal the original coating.

The Panama supply angle

Here is the one genuine structural advantage of building this way in this country, and almost nobody writes about it.

Container prices are set less by the steel than by the trucking. Boxes pile up where ships unload and get scarce inland, which is why the same unit swings a thousand dollars between a port city and a landlocked one. Panama sits at the end of the Canal with major container terminals on both coasts and the Colón Free Zone next door. If you are buying used boxes, you are shopping in one of the best-supplied markets in the hemisphere.

That advantage evaporates the moment your site is far from a road a truck-mounted crane can reach. A container is 12 metres long, weighs several tonnes empty, and cannot be carried in pieces. If the last kilometre to your lot is a dirt track with a switchback, price the crane and the road work before you price the box.

Should you build one here?

It is done, and done well it looks superb. But be clear-eyed about which problem you are solving.

If you want a container home because you love the industrial aesthetic and the modularity, that is a good reason, and a well-executed build with a ventilated over-roof, treated steel, closed-cell foam and real cross-ventilation will serve you for decades. If you want one because you heard it was the cheap way to build, the maths above should change your mind: you would be paying conventional-build money for a shell that fights the climate instead of working with it.

Our rule of thumb: containers suit small, modern, design-forward projects, guest cabins, studios and rentals. For a family home you plan to live in through twenty rainy seasons, ICF or plain reinforced concrete gets you there cheaper and cooler. The method comparison lays the options side by side.

Frequently asked questions

Are shipping container homes actually cheap?

The box is cheap. The house is not. A 40 ft one-trip runs $3,500 to $5,500, delivery adds $800 to $2,500, and insulation, openings and systems add another 30 to 60%, so budget $9,000 to $14,000 per box before foundation, permits or interior finish. Finished, a container home lands at $140 to $280 per square foot, right alongside a small conventional build.

What is the difference between cargo-worthy and one-trip?

Cargo-worthy means a maritime surveyor certified the box structurally sound for ocean shipping. It is typically 8 to 15 years old with surface rust and dents. One-trip means it has made a single voyage from the factory, with clean paint and no corrosion history. One-trip costs 40 to 60% more, and for a home that premium is usually worth paying.

Do container homes get too hot in the tropics?

Yes, unless the design attacks solar gain from the outside. Adding R-value inside helps, but the bigger lever is a ventilated over-roof plus high-reflectance, high-emissivity exterior coatings that cut peak steel temperature. Then cross-ventilation and shading. Bare steel in direct equatorial sun is not a building, it is a kiln.

Do shipping containers rust in humid climates?

They do, and it matters more than for other buildings because the steel skin is the structure rather than a cladding. Coastal salt air accelerates it. Treat and coat every surface, keep water off the roof and out of the corner castings, and inspect on a schedule you actually keep.

Is spray foam the right insulation for a container?

Closed-cell, yes, and never open-cell in humid air, because open-cell absorbs moisture and loses effectiveness. The important caveat is what is underneath it. Foam sprayed over untreated or rusting steel seals the corrosion in and hides it while it spreads. Treat and coat the steel first, and specify low-VOC foam, since many containers carry toxic marine paints.
